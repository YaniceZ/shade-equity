# Open global data underestimate pedestrian shade and misstate older adults' sun exposure on routes to essential services

**Yanice Zeng** · Independent researcher, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada

*Draft, 19 November 2026. Not peer reviewed. References were checked against the original sources on 21 November 2026.*

## Abstract

Shade on the walk to a store, clinic or bus stop is one of the few heat protections that a city controls, and older adults, who walk slowly and are most at risk in extreme heat, depend on it. Global open datasets now make it possible to model shade in any city, but their accuracy at the scale of a sidewalk is unknown. We modelled direct-sun shading on 4,044 km of walkways in Vancouver, Canada, every 30 minutes from 10:00 to 17:00 on 28 June 2021, the peak of the Pacific Northwest heat dome. We used two obstacle models: airborne LiDAR, and GlobalBuildingAtlas building heights combined with the Meta/WRI 1 m global canopy height map. We then routed residents to the nearest of six types of essential destination. LiDAR gave 45.2% of walkway shaded over the day (41.0% counting only green canopy) and the global data 28.5%. Almost all of the gap came from trees (−17.9 percentage points), with buildings contributing +1.0. Neighbourhood rankings agreed (Spearman ρ = 0.84), but threshold indicators did not: 16.8% of residents aged 65 and over had a grocery store within a 10-minute walk on a route at least half shaded at 15:00 by LiDAR, compared with 4.4% by global data. Older adults' routes were neither shadier nor sunnier than those of other residents. Older adults spent more time in direct sun mainly because they walk more slowly (+44%) and, for clinics, pharmacies and groceries, because they live slightly farther away (+5%). In simulation, 50 km of street trees placed where older adults walk reduced their sun exposure by 10.6%, seven times more than the same length planted on the sunniest walkways. WorldPop's 2025 age layers assign one 65+ share to all of Vancouver, so they cannot locate older adults within the city. Global data are adequate for ranking neighbourhoods but not for absolute shade targets, which need local canopy data.

## Introduction

Extreme heat is the deadliest weather hazard in North America, and older adults carry most of the burden. During the 2021 heat dome the BC Coroners Service identified 619 heat-related deaths, about two-thirds of them among people aged 70 and over¹. Almost all of these deaths (98%) happened indoors¹, but older adults still have to go out for groceries, medicine, appointments and transit, and on those trips shade from trees and buildings is the main protection that city planning can provide. Shade cuts the direct solar load on a pedestrian and is the largest single control on mean radiant temperature outdoors.

Recent work has moved shade analysis from tree-canopy cover to the pedestrian network. Gu et al. simulated sidewalk shade at 0.5 m in nine cities and found that lower-income and peripheral neighbourhoods receive less shade². Wolf et al. measured how much shade-seeking routing can reduce sun exposure in Manhattan, Barcelona and Valencia³. Duran-Sala et al. showed with mobile-phone flows that the places people travel to, not only where they live, shape heat exposure by age and income⁴. We found no city-wide study of how much sun older adults meet on their routes to everyday destinations.

Two questions remain open. First, these studies rely on high-resolution surface models that most cities do not have: Gu et al. used the Google Solar API digital surface model, which is not openly licensed, and national LiDAR for Amsterdam². Global open datasets of building heights⁵ and canopy height⁶ could extend shade analysis to any city, and products such as the WRI Cool Cities Lab already use them⁷, but how well they reproduce sidewalk shade, and whether their errors change equity conclusions, has not been tested. Second, shade inequality has been measured as an area average; what matters for an older pedestrian is the shade on the actual route to the places they need to reach, walked at their own pace.

We address both questions in Vancouver, where open LiDAR, a densely mapped sidewalk network and dissemination-area census counts are available. We ask (1) how closely open global data reproduce LiDAR-based walkway shade, and whether the error comes from buildings or trees; (2) whether those errors change rankings and threshold-based equity indicators; (3) how much time older adults spend in direct sun on routes to essential destinations compared with other residents, and why; and (4) how much targeted street-tree planting could reduce that exposure, as a scenario simulation.

## Methods

**Study day and sun positions.** Shade was modelled for 28 June 2021, the day of Vancouver's highest temperatures during the heat dome, every 30 minutes from 10:00 to 17:00 local time (15 time steps). Solar positions were computed with the NOAA general solar position equations at the city centroid. A second day (29 July 2021) was used as a sensitivity check.

**Obstacle models.** All rasters were placed on one 1 m grid in UTM zone 10N covering the city plus 600 m (226 million cells). Both models share the LiDAR terrain (NRCan HRDEM, BC Lower Mainland 2016 project, 1 m DTM), so differences come only from buildings and trees.

- *Reference (LiDAR).* The normalised surface (DSM − DTM, same project) was split with Overture building footprints (169,987 footprints from OpenStreetMap, City of Vancouver and Microsoft sources): cells inside footprints and at least 2 m tall are buildings; cells outside a 1 m dilation of the footprints and at least 2.5 m tall are trees.
- *Global.* Building heights from the GlobalBuildingAtlas LoD1 product⁵ (157,583 footprints, all with heights) were rasterised; trees are Meta/WRI canopy heights⁶ of at least 3 m outside a 1 m dilation of those footprints.
- *Factorial swaps.* Two hybrid models (LiDAR buildings with global trees, and the reverse) separate the error due to each layer.

**Shadow casting.** For each sample point and time step a ray was marched from the ground towards the sun in 1 m steps. Terrain and buildings are opaque. A tree blocks the ray only between its crown base (25% of tree height, the UMEP default⁸) and its top, and passes 10% of the direct beam, following Gu et al.². The output is the sunlit fraction of the direct beam (1, 0.1 or 0). The algorithm is implemented in Numba and tested against analytic wall-shadow lengths.

**Walkways.** The walk network was built from OpenStreetMap (Geofabrik extract of 17 November 2026) with pyrosm. In Vancouver, sidewalks are mapped as separate footways: 2,751 km of the 4,044 km of walkway inside the city are footways, which we sampled on their own line. Streets without separately mapped sidewalks were sampled on both sides, offset from the centreline by half a carriageway width by road class plus 1.5 m; lanes were sampled on the centreline. Points were placed every 2 m (2.39 million points). Points inside building footprints were dropped. Shade on a segment is the mean over its points.

**Population.** WorldPop 2025 constrained 100 m population was used for residential locations. Its age-structure layers assign a single 65+ share (18.34%) to almost every cell in Vancouver, so the count of residents aged 65 and over in each cell was taken instead from the 2021 Census Profile for the dissemination area containing the cell centre (1,019 dissemination areas; 113,120 residents aged 65+, 17.0%), retrieved through the Statistics Canada SDMX service.

**Destinations and routes.** Six destination types were taken from OpenStreetMap: grocery stores (supermarket, greengrocer, grocery), pharmacies, clinics and doctors (including hospitals), transit stops and stations, parks (at least 0.2 ha; any network node within 20 m counts as an entrance), and community centres and libraries. For each type, a multi-source Dijkstra search on walking distance gives every network node its nearest destination and shortest path. Sunlit length was accumulated along each path for every time step. Older adults were assumed to walk at 0.9 m/s and other adults at 1.3 m/s. Indicators were weighted by residents aged 65+ or by all residents: minutes in direct sun per one-way trip at 15:00; whether the nearest destination is within 10 minutes' walk; and whether that route is at least half shaded at 15:00. Shade-seeking routes minimise length + α × sunlit length for α = 0.5–4³. Uncertainty was estimated by resampling 1 km spatial blocks of origin cells (300 replicates).

**Street-tree scenarios.** Older adults' shortest paths were loaded onto the network to give the number of older adults routed over each segment. Two rules select segments up to a budget of 10, 25 or 50 km of walkway: *targeted* (sunlit length at 15:00 × older adults routed) and *sunniest* (sunlit share at 15:00). Trees 8 m tall with 3 m crown radius were added every 8 m along the selected walkways, shade was recomputed, and older adults' mean sun-exposed minutes per trip were compared with the baseline. These are scenario simulations with a fixed tree geometry, not predictions of the effect of a planting programme.

**Validation of the LiDAR reference.** Of tall LiDAR objects outside footprints in the city, 87% lie in 10 m Sentinel-2 cells with NDVI ≥ 0.3 (scene of 15 July 2018, which covers the whole city). To identify the remaining 13%, we compared them with the City of Vancouver's classified 2022 point cloud in four 1 km tiles selected for having the most non-green objects (one residential, one downtown, one port and one industrial). In each 1 m cell we took the class of the highest 2022 point at least 2.5 m above the 2022 ground. Of the 2016 green canopy, 74% was classed as vegetation in 2022 and 12% had no tall object, which reflects tree removal and misregistration over six years. Of the non-green objects, 14% were vegetation, 14% building, 39% unclassified (vehicles, containers, poles and other structures) and 34% had no tall object. The share that was vegetation ranged from 35% in the residential tile to 2% in the port tile. Most non-green objects are therefore not trees. A classification by height texture, which labelled most of them as crowns, did not agree with the point cloud and is reported only as a sensitivity test. We therefore treat the green-only reference as a conservative lower bound and all tall objects as an upper bound.

## Results

**Open global data underestimate walkway shade, and trees cause it.** Across 4,044 km of walkway, LiDAR gave a daytime shaded share of 45.2% and the global model 28.5% (Fig. 2c, d). The gap was present at every hour, from 49.8% versus 33.0% at 10:00 to 40.8% versus 24.0% near solar noon. Swapping one layer at a time, global trees reduced city shade by 17.9 percentage points and global buildings increased it by 1.0 (interaction +0.1; Fig. 2e). GlobalBuildingAtlas heights matched LiDAR closely for the 96% of buildings under 10 m (bias +0.7 m), but were 1.8 m too low for 10–30 m buildings and 18.7 m too low for the 784 buildings over 30 m (Fig. 2a). These towers are concentrated downtown and have little weight in a citywide average. The Meta/WRI map covered 18.1% of non-building land in the city with canopy, compared with 37.3% for all tall LiDAR objects and 31.7% for green LiDAR canopy only. It found 45% of the green LiDAR canopy, and 63% of crowns at least 8 m tall (Fig. 2b), so the problem is as much where the global canopy is as how much there is.

**Rankings are preserved; absolute values are not.** At the segment level the global model explained little of the LiDAR variation (R² = 0.12, Spearman ρ = 0.64; MAE 0.21). At 15:00, 68% of sample points had the same shade class in both models (κ = 0.36), and the global model found 48% of LiDAR-shaded points. Averaging removed much of the scatter: the 22 neighbourhoods were ranked similarly (ρ = 0.84) and so were 209 hexagons of 500 m (ρ = 0.83), and the five least-shaded neighbourhoods (Sunset, Victoria-Fraserview, Renfrew-Collingwood, Hastings-Sunrise and Kensington-Cedar Cottage) were the same set in both. The bias, however, stayed near −17 points everywhere (Fig. 2f). Indicators that compare shade with a threshold were therefore strongly affected. For example, the share of older adults whose nearest grocery store is within 10 minutes on a route at least half shaded at 15:00 was 16.8% (95% CI 13.5–20.2%) with LiDAR and 4.4% (3.2–5.5%) with global data (Fig. 3c).

**Older adults' exposure is driven by pace and distance.** At 15:00, a one-way trip to the nearest clinic exposed residents aged 65+ to 9.0 minutes of direct sun (95% CI 8.2–10.0) by LiDAR and 11.9 minutes by global data. The figures for other destinations were 7.9 (pharmacy), 7.4 (grocery), 7.9 (community centre), 3.2 (park) and 2.4 minutes (transit stop) (Fig. 3b). The global model overstated exposure by about 30% for every destination type, but ranked neighbourhoods almost identically by older adults' sun minutes (ρ = 0.93). Route shade did not differ between older adults and all residents: the difference was within ±0.4 percentage points for all six destination types, and the 65+ share of a cell was not correlated with its route shade (ρ = 0.03). The extra exposure of older adults came from two sources (Fig. 3d). Walking at 0.9 rather than 1.3 m/s adds 44% more time in the sun on the same route. For clinics, pharmacies and grocery stores, older adults also live about 5% farther away than other residents (for example 723 versus 685 m to the nearest grocery store). Distance alone overstates access: of older adults with a destination within 10 minutes, 56–63% (depending on destination type) face a route that is mostly in direct sun at 15:00.

**Targeting matters more than amount.** Shade-seeking routes reduced older adults' sun exposure by 21% for 1.1% extra distance (α = 0.5) and by 29% for 7.6% extra distance (α = 4; Fig. 4a). In the planting scenario, 50 km of new street trees on the sunny walkways that older adults use most reduced their mean sun exposure per trip from 6.32 to 5.65 minutes (−10.6%). Planting the same length on the sunniest walkways reduced it by 1.5% (Fig. 4b). The targeted segments are spread across the city (Fig. 4c).

**Robustness.** Raising crown transmissivity to 30% lowered both estimates (36.2% and 23.5%) but left the gap at −12.7 points. Solid crowns, street centrelines instead of sidewalks, and a later study day changed the gap by less than one point. The green-only reference, a lower bound, gave 41.0% shade; against it the global model still underestimated walkway shade by 12.5 points and placed it poorly at the segment level (MAE 0.17, ρ = 0.70). The bias of the global data is therefore between 12.5 and 16.7 percentage points.

## Discussion

Global open datasets are attractive for shade analysis because they cover every city. In Vancouver they reproduce the ranking of neighbourhoods, and therefore support prioritising areas, but they underestimate walkway shade by about a third. Almost all of the error comes from the canopy layer. Urban trees, especially street trees and crowns over pavement, are hard to map from satellite imagery. Global building heights, despite large errors for towers, matter much less for shade on most walkways because most walkways are in low-rise neighbourhoods. This has a practical consequence: any absolute target based on global data, such as a minimum shade share on routes to clinics, would show a much larger shortfall than really exists and would misplace it at the scale of individual blocks.

Our second finding concerns what kind of inequality older adults face. Gu et al. found that low-income areas receive less sidewalk shade². In Vancouver we found no age gradient in route shade. Older adults' disadvantage instead comes from slower walking and slightly longer distances, both of which turn the same shade deficit into more minutes of exposure. Interventions that focus on routes older adults actually use, rather than on canopy averages, are much more effective per kilometre in our simulations.

Third, population data choice matters. WorldPop age layers are widely used for vulnerable-population heat maps, including the elderly layer of the WRI Cool Cities Lab⁷. In Vancouver they distribute the 65+ population exactly in proportion to total population, so any within-city comparison of older adults and others based on them is uninformative. We do not know how general this is; it should be checked for other countries before such layers are used for within-city equity analysis.

**Limitations.** Shade is a proxy for, not a measure of, thermal comfort; air temperature, humidity and long-wave radiation from hot surfaces are not modelled. The LiDAR (2016), canopy imagery (mostly 2018–2020), buildings and population (2021–2026) are from different years, and part of the difference between models is real change, such as tree loss and redevelopment. The size of the global-data bias depends on how much of the non-green LiDAR signal (13% of tall objects) is canopy: it is 12.5 points against a green-only reference and 16.7 points against all tall objects. A check against the classified 2022 point cloud in four tiles suggests most of that signal is not canopy, so the lower figure is closer to the truth; a citywide check was not possible because the city's tiles must be downloaded one at a time by hand. Routing assumes the nearest destination and the shortest path. Census counts were allocated to 100 m cells in proportion to WorldPop totals within each dissemination area. Results come from one temperate city with a dense mapped sidewalk network and should be tested in hotter cities and in cities where sidewalks are not mapped.
